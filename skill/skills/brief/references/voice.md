# Voice and readability

Read this before writing. It is the difference between a briefing and a report.

## Three depths

A reader can stop at any depth and come away correctly informed:
1. **Five seconds: what happened.** `headline` and `what_happened` give the day's concrete
   events in plain news language, with no interpretation. Under the article, the page lists
   every kept story (code builds that).
2. **The read: what it means.** The `lead` and the `sections` are one article. Almost all your
   words go here.
3. **The evidence.** Links, threads, numbers and attributions sit in the data beside the prose,
   so the prose stays prose.

## Headlines lead with the event

Every heading names the concrete event in roughly its first six words, then the interpretation.
- Write "DeepSeek raises $15B days after US restrictions: export controls aren't slowing China's
  open labs".
- Not "Export controls aren't slowing China's open labs".
- The test: could a reader tell what happened from the heading alone?

## One article, not a stack of blocks

- **The lead** states the day's single through-line in 2–4 tight paragraphs. Open on the most
  important concrete fact, not an abstraction. Events earn the thesis.
- **Sections develop it,** one theme each, usually 1–4 paragraphs. Connect them with real
  transitions ("the same squeeze shows up one layer down…") and call back to the lead. A reader
  should feel one mind moving through the day.
- **Put a minor-but-real story where it belongs:** a sentence in the related section, not a
  section of its own.
- **End when the argument lands.** No summary paragraph.

## The reader: an informed peer

- **They follow AI.** Don't explain benchmarks, open weights, RL, MoE, context windows, agents,
  inference costs or the major labs.
- **Give a few words to what a peer might not know:** a new method's name, a small lab, a niche
  benchmark, an acronym from another field.
- **Say why it matters in concrete terms:** what changed, for whom, and what to watch or try
  because of it.
- **Plain words still beat jargon,** and an insider's shorthand still needs to land.

## Voice

- **Lead with the concrete.** Open paragraphs on the specific fact, number, name or result. The
  generalization is the payoff, not the setup.
- **Vary the rhythm.** Mix developed sentences with short ones. A one-line paragraph is fine
  when a point earns it.
- **Use strong verbs and plain words, in one confident voice.** State opinions as opinions: "my
  read is", "I'd bet". You're an advisor, not a wire service.
- **Cut hedging and filler:** "it's worth noting", "arguably", "in many ways", "that said",
  "interestingly", "notably", "it remains to be seen".
- **Calibrate honestly, in one clause:** how sure you are, and what would change your mind. Say
  it once, where it matters.
- **Turn disagreement into depth.** Where the evidence or the commenters split, give the richer
  take. Use a section's `split` (for, against, your read) only for a genuinely two-sided call,
  at most once or twice a brief.
- **Attribute discussion plainly:** "a Hacker News commenter who maintains a PHP framework",
  "LessWrong readers pushed back". Use the quote itself only when the exact words matter.

## Length

- **A normal day is a 10-minute read:** 1,500–2,500 words across the lead, sections and updates.
- **A big day can run a little longer.** A quiet day should be shorter, and say it's quiet.
- **`what_happened`:** 3–6 items, one clause each.
- **`headline`:** one line.
